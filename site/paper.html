Xeric: An Architecture for Persistent,
Locally-Hosted Simulated Communities

Mr. Gwonk

gwonk@xeric.dev · August 2, 2026
Preprint. The software artifact is the repository itself [1].

Abstract

We describe Xeric, a working engine for persistent simulated communities that run on a user's own hardware. A one-time construction phase (the forge) interviews the user and directs a capable language model through a sequence of validated passes that produce a world as data: places with hours and coordinates, a cast (twelve by default) with weekly schedules, homes, interiors, and secrets, and a set of typed knowledge walls declaring who may know what. The world then runs indefinitely on a small local model. A once-a-minute heartbeat process lives unattended hours: sweeps write offscreen events whose participants receive deliberately divergent memories, characters initiate contact, and a world clock — real time plus a stored offset — supports pausing, bounded skips, and historical start dates.

The contribution is architectural rather than algorithmic. We present five disciplines that together make a persistent world tractable on consumer hardware: (i) a byte-stable prompt prefix layout that preserves the inference server's cache across turns; (ii) knowledge walls as typed deny rules that fail closed, consumed by renderers and never re-derived; (iii) an age floor enforced by structural exclusion rather than instruction; (iv) per-witness memory divergence, measured and enforced, as the mechanism of subjectivity; and (v) a recorded decision trail that makes every generated hour inspectable after the fact. We additionally describe a story overlay format whose closure is a subtraction — the composed world returns byte-identical to its pre-story state — and a rewind semantics in which the world forgets an undone week while the player does not. The engine is implemented in approximately 50,000 lines of dependency-free PHP over SQLite and is exercised by twelve test suites comprising 1,653 assertions.

1  Introduction

Language-model character applications are, almost universally, request–response systems: a static character card, a transcript, and a model asked to continue it. Nothing happens between requests. Continuity — who knows what, who promised what, who was where on Tuesday — is delegated to the model's attention over a growing transcript, and degrades accordingly. The resulting failure modes are familiar: characters who know things they were never told, worlds in which nothing occurs unless the user causes it, and casts whose knowledge is one shared pool wearing several names.

Xeric starts from a different premise: the world is data, and the model is a renderer of last resort. What may happen offscreen is chosen by the engine from a typed catalogue the world's construction armed; who may participate is resolved from schedules and place hours; who may know a fact is decided by declared walls before a prompt is assembled. The model is asked only to fill in shapes the world already permits, in the voice of one person, through that person's walls. When generation fails a structural check — two witnesses to one event returning what is effectively a single memory — the output is refused rather than repaired.

The system is deliberately local-first. Construction is the one phase permitted to be expensive, and may use any OpenAI-compatible endpoint or a local model; the runtime is engineered to be comfortable on a small model served from a single consumer GPU. No account, no telemetry, no cloud dependency at runtime: a world is a JSON template and a SQLite database on the owner's disk. This paper describes the system as implemented in the repository at the date above. It reports no user study and makes no benchmark claims; where numbers appear they are counts from the tree or its test suites.

A world on disk is a directory of separated concerns:

Table 1. The unit of ownership is the directory. The template is read on every turn; the seeded past and the lived past are indistinguishable after launch, by design.

The lifecycle has two phases with sharply different cost profiles. The forge (§3) runs once and is allowed minutes of a capable model's time; the runtime serves every subsequent turn from a small model whose prompt is engineered to cache (§4). Between visits, a heartbeat — one pass of a script every sixty seconds, run by the launcher, a cron entry, or systemd — lives the hours nobody is watching (§8.2). The engine proper is world-agnostic: anything that exists only because strangers might share one GPU (sessions, queues, rate limits) lives in the web layer, never in the engine, and costs nothing when the system runs privately.

3  World construction

3.1  The interview and the passes

Construction begins with a data-driven interview: scale, identity, theme, motivation, social shape, rating, and exclusions. Every question offers preset answers, free text, and a “surprise me” that draws one coherent concept rather than independent per-field rolls — a constraint the test suite asserts, since mixing two concepts produces worlds that are nothing in particular. The single most consequential answer is motivation (“why are you here?”): it selects which of 29 named subsystems the world arms — attraction, standings, rumors, scarcity, gentle proactive contact, and so on — and thereby which kinds of offscreen event can ever occur (§7). Free-text motivations are resolved onto the same vocabulary by the model, with a keyword fallback so a dead endpoint still yields a coherent world.

The forge then runs an ordered sequence of model passes, each validated before its output is load-bearing:

PassProduces
ConceptName, locale, era, texture, canon rules, mood axis
PlacesBusinesses and rooms: kind, hours, aliases, grid coordinates
CastOne call per character: voice, week, psyche, tells, drives, secrets (twelve by default; four for quick test worlds)
AssemblyOrbits, circles, economies, boons — code, not model output
WallsWho must never know what; protected roles (the safety-critical pass)
ProtagonistWhose story this is, when the user steps out of the centre
HomesHouseholds appended to the place list, so off-shift hours resolve to a real kitchen table
SeedWeeks of backfill: events and per-character memories, in relative time

Table 2. The pass sequence of xeric_forge_build(). Later passes see earlier output; homes land before seed so seeded history can name a real room. The repository's quickstart documents two to four minutes end-to-end on a local model [3].

The failure discipline is uniform: a pass returning unparseable output is retried once with the parse error fed back, then replaced by a deterministic hand-written default for that section. A world is always launchable; what happened is recorded in build notes the review step shows honestly. Prompts are written to a deliberately low floor — a 12B-parameter model must produce an acceptable world — by asking for flat keys with small values and assembling all nesting in code. A locally-forged world is treated as a legitimate world, not a degraded one.

3.2  Deterministic naming registers

An empirical observation motivated one unusual component. Each forge pass is a separate model call with no memory of any other world, so every world independently converges on the statistically safest tokens: in one eight-world sample from development, the same invented surname appeared in eight of eight casts, and four surnames covered 27 of 32 cast members. The mitigation is to assign diversity and then guarantee it deterministically. A naming register — one of thirty hand-written cultural banks — is selected per world from the world's own answers (never the date), and every pass that invents a proper noun carries it. Deterministic gates on each reply replace known repeat-offenders, and a read of the worlds already on disk gives the forge its only cross-world memory: the third world in a directory cannot reuse the first world's names, because those names are sitting right there. Names the user's own premise contains are exempt — the user already chose them.

3.3  Review before launch

The forge proposes; the user disposes. The review step exposes every generated section with per-section reroll, 159 editable fields, and — critically — every knowledge wall written out in plain English. The design rule is stated in the repository as: a hallucinated wall is a real secret told to the wrong person, and only the person who built the world can catch it. Hand-retyped corrections in review are also the highest-grade signal the learning layer ever receives (§9), being the one correction in the system that is not an inference.

4  Prompt assembly under a prefix-cache discipline

Local inference is cheap only while the server's prefix cache survives: every token before the first changed byte is reused between turns, and one moved byte near the top forces a full re-read of the world on every message. The prompt assembler therefore splits the world into a byte-stable prefix and a volatile tail, and the split is the entire design:

SYSTEM MESSAGE — static, fixed order, byte-stable across turns
  1. her voice                (who she is, how she sounds)
  2. how she answers          (format and register rules)
  3. the bible                (the world AS SHE SEES IT — walls applied)
  4. the economies she can see
  5. what this world has learned   (capped lessons, §9)
  6. what a story has left her holding   (changes only on beat open/spill)
  7. what she remembers       (LAST: the only static block that grows)

CONVERSATION — real chat turns, appended monotonically

LAST USER MESSAGE — volatile tail, rebuilt every turn
  the clock · the phase of day · where she is standing ·
  who else is in the room · who is dead · how long since
  you last spoke (world time) · per-turn coaching

Figure 1. Prompt layout. Memories sit last among the static blocks because everything that grows must sit after everything that does not, or the growth invalidates the blocks above it. The volatile facts ride the bottom of the final message — which is also where a model pays the most attention.

The discipline produces non-obvious placements. A death is a durable world fact and belongs in the bible — but it is the one durable fact that can arrive between two messages of a live conversation, so the dead are announced in the volatile tail, where a character cannot go on speaking of somebody in the present tense because her system message was assembled ten minutes ago. Conversely, the age floor (§6) is static: a constraint that never changes must never move between turns. The governing question, stated in the file header, is: can this change between two messages of one conversation? If it can, it goes in the tail. An inspector page reconstructs the exact assembled messages section-by-section with sizes, and verifies its reconstruction byte-for-byte against the real prompt (§7.3).

4.1  The anatomy of a mind

A character has no persistent process; the mind is re-assembled at every turn from typed stores, and its architecture is legible in the assembly order. It is worth describing as a cognitive stack, because each layer corresponds to a mechanism that exists in the code today rather than to an aspiration.

layer            block                   store it reads             cadence
-----            -----                   --------------             -------
self             YOU ARE (voice,         own cast record            static
                 interiors, pronouns)
disposition      sampler temperature     character.temperature      static
conscience       HOW YOU ANSWER          rules + rating style       static
worldview        the bible               template, through walls    static
habit            lessons                 learn.php notebook         slow (days)
obligation       WHAT YOU ARE OWED       expect.* arcs              transitional
episode          memories (newest N)     memories table             per harvest
perception       RIGHT NOW               clock, presence, deaths    volatile
temporality      "you last spoke ..."    newest message world-epoch volatile

Figure 3. The assembled mind, top of the system message to the bottom of the volatile tail. Static layers are byte-stable and ride the prefix cache; transitional layers change only at state transitions; volatile layers ride the last user message and change every turn.

The self is unwalled; the world is not. A speaker's own interior — voice, psyche, what soothes them, what pulls at them — renders from their own record in a YOU ARE block to which no wall ever applies: one always knows oneself. Everyone else's interior arrives only through the bible, after the walls have taken what this viewer may not have. This split is why a “protected” character still functions: they lose knowledge about others, never about themselves.

The subconscious is a rendering discipline, not a data structure. The engine's rule — stated in the prompt and enforced by what the prompt withholds — is that a character never narrates their own or anyone else's insides. What the interior drives instead is stage direction: when a promise is missed, the wounded party's prompt says, verbatim, that it “stung more than you let on” and that they never narrate this; it shows in small ways — what your hands do, what you leave unsaid, how long you take to answer. The feeling steers behavior without ever becoming text about feelings, and the durable ledger it moves — a trust integer per relationship — is adjusted silently and printed nowhere. The public record diverges from the private one by construction: the event row for a missed promise states only what a bystander could see (“kept half an eye on the door; left later than usual”), while the sting lives exclusively in the prompt of the person who felt it. Anything downstream that spreads information — gossip in design, the book view in code — therefore spreads observables, never interiors.

Episodic memory is harvested for change, not coverage. Every few exchanges a second, cheap model call reads the recent conversation and writes at most three sentences the character “would still know in a week,” under an instruction to prefer what changed — admitted, promised, refused, learned — over the fact that something was said; without that preference a memory block degenerates into a transcript of itself within twenty turns. Offscreen hours write memory the same way but per witness: one event produces a different sentence for each participant, which is the engine's mechanism of subjectivity (§7.2). Memories are dated in world time, and the harvest is also where commitments enter the obligation layer: the model reports the user's verbatim words, and code — never the model — decides whether they constituted a promise.

Temporal orientation is supplied, not inferred. A transcript is undated, so the volatile tail states the gap outright — “you two last spoke four days ago,” day-coarse, world-clock — while a static rule instructs that a quiet stretch is ordinary life and not an event. The pairing matters: the first line alone invites invented grievance, the second alone leaves the model doing calendar arithmetic it reliably gets wrong. Obligations render at the same coarseness (“you are expecting them Saturday”), never as countdowns, both because nobody texting a friend counts minutes and because a timer would change every turn and forfeit the cache.

Dreams are recombination under a wall check. During the world's dream window a character with standing may wake and text about a dream assembled only from material already theirs — their own dated memories and the commons prose of hours they attended, at most three fragments, none invented. The prompt asks for dream logic: familiar people and places recombined slightly wrong, no plot, no revelation. The finished text is then scanned against every protected fact in the world and refused whole on contact, on the argument that a dream which reveals a secret is a wall breach wearing pajamas. An empty material list produces no dream rather than a synthetic one — the mind does not confabulate at 3 a.m. any more than it does at noon.

What the world learns about its person lives outside any one mind. The signals layer records what the user actually did — who they answered, whose message they left sitting, what they retyped by hand — and distils it into per-kind weights with floors (nothing is ever switched off, so the world retains the capacity to surprise) and a capped notebook of plain-language lessons that ride every character's prompt. The distil pass may also strike a lesson the recent record contradicts, at most one per pass, so a habit abandoned in week two does not steer week eight. Every layer described in this section is inspectable: the inspector reproduces the assembled mind byte for byte, section by section, with the stores and sizes named — the claim that a character's behavior has legible causes is checkable on any turn of any world.

5  Knowledge walls

5.1  Typed deny rules, consumed not re-derived

Shared canon leaks by default. The expensive way to learn this — recorded in the code's own comments — is to hand a protected relative the same world-bible as everybody else. Xeric's rule is that renderers consume walls and never re-derive them: every audience-restricted fact declares its audience in the template, as a wall with a key, an audience selector, a list of hidden paths drawn from a fixed vocabulary of about thirty (secrets, drives.<handle>, economies.<key>, mystery.room, …), and a shown_as framing written in the second person for the walled viewer to read instead. Hiding is one-directional and subtree-scoped: hiding one ledger removes that ledger and leaves the section standing.

RESOLVING WHAT ONE VIEWER IS TOLD
  1. resolve the viewer: character → fixture → unresolved
       · unresolved handles get the commons only, at the weakest
         rating (fail closed)
       · a null viewer is the narrator: full canon, walls off
  2. attach special_role walls named for this handle directly
  3. match audience selectors {role, orbit, circle, handle},
       conjunctively; an unknown selector field never matches
  4. if the role carries own_bible: append a synthetic floor wall
       denying the intimate layers wholesale (dossiers, secrets,
       drives, psyche, tells, protagonist, mystery.room) —
       a deny-by-default posture no declared wall has to remember
  5. render: every section asks xeric_hidden(walls, path)
       before writing a word

Figure 2. Wall resolution order. Load-time validation separately rejects the silent failure modes — a character in an undeclared orbit falls outside every wall audience, and a wall aimed at a role nobody holds protects nobody; both are reported loudly at load rather than discovered in a leak.

The fail-closed posture is systemic: an unresolvable viewer receives the commons at the weakest rating; an unparseable rating ranks as the weakest and can never un-gate anything; an unknown wall selector never matches. The single deliberate exception is documented as such: an unreadable permadeath setting resolves to revivable, because the age floor protects a third party while permadeath is a constraint an author places on themselves, and losing a cast to a typo is damage, not caution.

5.2  Leak detection in loose prose

Walls govern structured rendering, but prose leaks: a seeded memory, a lesson, an event title can restate a protected fact in different words. A shared six-word-run quotation test (xeric_wall_quotes()) compares loose prose against every wall's explanation and every protected role's must_not_know; the forge checks its own seed output against it and drops offending lines rather than rewriting them, and story overlays inherit the same check for free.

5.3  The narrator: discretion as an output-side wall

The engine has carried a wall-less viewer since the first day of the renderer — rendering the bible with a null viewer yields full canon — and the narrator subsystem gives that viewer a mouth: a read-only oracle the world's author can ask why. Its design distinguishes two fences with different failure modes. Authorial knowledge — a story's solution, a red herring's falsity, an unspilled piece, a death's perpetrator — is absent from the prompt entirely, not fenced by instruction: instruction-following is probabilistic and absence is arithmetic; a model that never read the culprit's name cannot leak it. In-world hidden facts — secrets, drives, wall payloads — are present under stated discretion, because answering “what has she been carrying around?” at the right altitude requires knowing what and whose it is. Citations in narrator answers are deterministic — a manifest of what was actually assembled, never claimed by the model — and the read-only property is enforced as a contract, with the test suite counting every row of every table before and after an ask.

6  Content rating and the age floor

A world carries a rating ceiling; a model endpoint carries its own; the effective rating is their minimum, so a wholesome template stays wholesome on any model and a permissive template degrades gracefully on a censored one. Sessions that have not affirmed adulthood are pinned to the floor tier. The ladder uses broadcast tiers, on the observation that television already taught everyone to read them, and each tier carries a house style — a rating that only gated content pools would change what may appear without changing how anything reads:

KeyRankLabelProse stance (abridged)
sfw0TV-GWarm, plain; trouble is casseroles, grudges, weather, pride
pg1TV-PGConflict and loss happen; the camera cuts away
teen2TV-14Real stakes and menace; the wound is implied
mature3TV-MAAdult lives on screen; grim allowed, gratuitous lazy
explicit4UnratedNothing off the table; explicitness serves the scene

Table 3. The rating ladder. Unknown strings rank 0: an unreadable rating must never un-gate anything. Any template node may carry a rating_min floor below which it simply does not exist.

The age floor is the sharpest instance of the architecture's preference for structure over instruction. Minority is derived, never declared: a character carries an integer age and no flag, because a flag is a field a model can write and therefore a field a model can clear. Every gate funnels through one predicate, which answers “minor” for anything it cannot read. A minor's effective rating is the weakest tier in every world whatever the world is rated; the desire economy is closed to minors structurally, at forge assembly and at every render, rather than by asking a model to behave. The rule is equally explicit about its own boundary: it gates sexual content and nothing else. A child in a world is an ordinary character — schedule, orbit, secrets, witness — and the repository treats a rule that would keep a child out of a scene, an hour, or a conversation as the rule written backwards. In the worked mystery fixture, a twelve-year-old holds the piece of evidence that collapses a red herring, which is the oldest working part of the genre placed where it belongs [5].

7  Offscreen life

7.1  Sweeps: shape and casting are not up to the model

A sweep converts an unlived hour into at most one event. Two decisions are made by the engine before any model is consulted. First, the shape: an event kind is drawn from a catalogue of eighteen (shared_meal, rumor, confidence, friction, glimpse, absence, …), each gated on the subsystems the forge armed — a world that never armed anything convivial never generates shared meals, however much the model would enjoy writing one. Second, the casting: participants come from presence resolution over weekly schedules, place hours, and homes, so the night clerk who is asleep does not have afternoon coffee with the mechanic who is under a truck. Events involving the spine of a protected secret exclude protected characters at casting time. Sweeps run on an hourly window and are idempotent per window — the guard is written whether the hour produced an event or was skipped — so any number of overlapping callers yields one hour's worth of life. A cadence parameter (default 0.35 per eligible window) keeps a lived-in week from becoming a soap opera.

7.2  Divergent memory as the mechanism of subjectivity

Each participant in an event receives their own memory of it, and the divergence between those memories is measured, not hoped for. Two accounts of one hour should share nouns; what the check catches is the failure where the model writes one sentence and reuses it — at which point the world has collapsed into an omniscient narrator wearing two hats, and every later conversation gives it away. Above a similarity threshold (0.72) the event is refused outright, which the code defends as a better failure than a flat world. A second threshold (0.75) rejects memories that merely echo something the same character already remembers. She remembers what he did with his hands; he remembers that she left early: subjectivity here is not a prompt style but an enforced property of the data.

7.3  Inspectability: the decision trail

Every sweep records its reasoning as it decides — which kinds this world could produce, which were tried, who was standing where, who was excluded and on what grounds, which grouping won and at what weight — keyed by the resulting event, so “why did that happen?” is answerable a week later. The same inspector answers “why did nothing happen?”, which the repository identifies as the actual question behind “why is my world so quiet?”. The other half of the inspector prints the exact prompt any character receives, split into its assembly sections with byte sizes, checked byte-for-byte against the real prompt — on the argument that an inspector showing something adjacent to the truth is worse than no inspector. The page refuses non-owners: it prints every secret verbatim, and cannot be made safe by redaction, only by refusal.

7.4  Proactive contact

Characters text first, on a ladder of occasions with distinct base rates — aftermath of an event, mid-event, a user missing for three days, dreams, diaries, undercurrents — under per-character and per-cast daily caps, with quiet hours respected. The transcript itself is undated, so the volatile tail states how long it has been since the two of you last spoke, in world time; without it, last month's goodnight abuts today's hello and the arithmetic lands on a model that never does it.

8  Time

8.1  The clock

World time is real time plus a stored offset, kept in the world's database so cron, web, and CLI agree without coordination. Three rules hold: the world does not run backwards (memories are stamped with world epochs, and no prose talks a model out of a date); one jump is bounded at seven days (a fat-finger fence, not a policy); and a paused world refuses to be advanced, because a skip while stopped would fabricate hours the world was not running for. Because everything downstream was written against real datetimes rather than an hour counter, a world may start at any date the platform can parse: a world set in 1873 New York reports its timezone as −04:56 — local mean time, standard zones not reaching the United States until 1883 — a correctness the repository notes nobody built on purpose.

8.2  The heart

Unattended life is delivered by a deliberately unambitious process: one pass, then exit, looped by the launcher or a crontab line. A single pass and death is crash-safe by construction and testable by running it once; idempotent windows mean a tick that finds no new hour does no work and costs no tokens, so nothing remembers when it last ran. Three refusals make it safe at one-minute cadence: it will not run a stopped world; it will not take the model from somebody mid-conversation (a person waiting on a reply outranks a town having a Tuesday — the window remains unswept and is simply still there next minute); and it will not live an unbounded gap in one tick — a month away is on the order of 240 model calls, so it lives at most six windows per world per tick and a long gap fills over several minutes.

8.3  Rewind: the world forgets, the player does not

Skips are real time passing: obligations fall due during them, and the residue lands as if the hours had been lived slowly. The mercy is the rewind, and its semantics are chosen for their physics: a rewind undoes the last skip only, whole — its events, memories, and firings un-happen, and the clock returns. It is destructive on purpose, and asymmetric on purpose: the world forgets the undone future; the player does not. One rewinds to make the missed appointment precisely because one remembers what happened when it was missed. No branches, no reach past the most recent skip.

9  Adaptation without drift

The world learns its user in two layers, and the ordering is the point. A counting layer needs no model and therefore always runs: per-character reply rates, per-kind engagement, reply lengths, tallied into weights that bend which kinds of hour the sweeps produce and who reaches out how often. A distillation layer, permitted to be absent, turns the raw signals — above all sentences the user retyped by hand in review — into at most six one-line lessons per character, deduplicated, riding the static prompt. Four rules keep learning from eating the world: lessons are capped (a notebook that grows forever eventually is the prompt); nothing is ever extinguished (every weight has a floor, because a world that only does what you liked last week has stopped being able to surprise you); a signal is read once; and learning is garnish — every write may fail quietly, because a chat turn that died for its own bookkeeping would be the tail wagging the dog.

10  Story overlays: a plot that ends over a world that does not

A story is a sidecar file composed onto the world at load, never written into the template. The central claim is testable and tested: closing a story is a subtraction, not an edit — compose, resolve, compose again, and the composed template returns byte-identical to the untouched one. Residue lives where a lived past belongs, in memory: the culprit remembers the Tuesday it came out. Multiple overlays compose in one direction only (walls accumulate; no overlay may remove another's), which makes them order-independent in the only respect where order would be dangerous.

The format's economy comes from an observation the repository states as engineering rather than metaphor: a mystery is a wall structure. One character knows the truth; several hold pieces (composed as secrets); not-knowing is a protection the sweeps already enforce; and pacing decides when the walls come down. Pacing is data — a piecewise-linear intensity curve with a mandated false calm, a window in which no beat may open and every multiplier is exactly 1.0: the story letting go for a stretch while the town carries on as if nobody had died, which is precisely the stretch generative systems otherwise get wrong by escalating forever or going slack. The curve modulates only two knobs that already exist — how often sweeps fire (0.4× to 1.6×) and the kind weights — and a multiplier is a thumb, never a gate: a story cannot arm a subsystem the world never armed, which is also what keeps the age floor's structural argument intact under overlays. The genuinely new mechanic is the red herring: a character who sincerely believes something false, whose falsity is engine-side knowledge that never enters their prompt — told it is mistaken, a model hedges, and hedging is the death of a wrong lead. Resolution requires the supporting beats to have actually been spilled: naming the right person on day one does nothing, because a guess is not a solution. A scratch validator run recorded in the design document executed 375 checks against the worked fixture and caught all ten deliberately-introduced authoring errors [5].

11  Designed, not yet implemented

Honesty about the boundary between running code and design is a stated norm of the repository, and the boundary moves quickly: the expectations construct, the dream rung, the rewind, the narrator's investigate power, and the two-party duet all crossed it on the day of writing and are described above as the running code they now are. What remains on the far side: the room — the duet's three-or-more generalization, whose design fixes who answers and who stays silent as the interesting questions, together with its watching surface (play, pause, walking in mid-scene); the remaining social constructs (gossip ripple, which the observables-only rule has made cheap, and confidences — a user-created wall whose betrayal is the highest-stakes event the design names); the narrator's write-ahead (intent as a pull, never a script) and its validated, undoable author mode. Player embodiment exists in early form: places carry grid coordinates and a world carries its size in minutes, so travel can cost world time; the design document argues the consequential feature is not the map but the travel time, since a frictionless choice is not a choice. Media generation ships no pipeline in this tree; the template merely carries the fields.

12  Status and evaluation

The engine runs end to end: forge, review, launch, play, heartbeat. The implementation is approximately 50,600 lines of PHP across 67 files, roughly 41,400 lines outside the test suites, with no runtime dependencies beyond PHP 8.1+ with SQLite; a bring-your-own model speaks the OpenAI chat API, locally or remote, and keys are never stored. Known limitations are stated plainly in the roadmap [7] and are visible within the first sessions: editing a launched world takes effect live and unannounced, while stored memories still describe who they were, and any prefix edit silently discards the cache; the undo in review is one step deep by design; a lesson never expires and is only ever pushed out by newer ones; and the inspector shows the lessons a world has learned but not the signal weights behind them. The engine has been developed and exercised on one machine in one house; no multi-user deployment at scale, no user study, and no formal evaluation of long-horizon coherence exist. The measure the project actually optimizes — whether a world still feels inhabited after months — is not one this paper can quantify.

13  Related concepts

The work sits, deliberately, at the engineering end of a line of ideas, which are discussed here in prose rather than cited; the paper claims no priority over any of them. Agent simulations in the “generative agents” tradition demonstrated memory streams, reflection, and daily schedules for language-model townspeople; Xeric shares the ambition and differs in commitments: knowledge separation is typed data enforced outside the model rather than an emergent property of retrieval, offscreen generation is gated by an armed-subsystem catalogue rather than open-ended planning, and the system is engineered to a single consumer GPU rather than a research budget. Prefix caching in local inference servers is the load-bearing external fact of §4; the contribution there is not the cache but the discipline of byte-stable world rendering that keeps it warm. Structured-output techniques motivate the forge's contract of flat keys and code-side assembly. The one-viewpoint-per-call rule (§5) — a room of N speakers is N calls, because one call means one point of view and every wall collapses into it silently — is stated in the roadmap as a constraint not open to revisiting, and functions as the system's rejection of the multi-character single-prompt shortcut common in practice.

14  Availability

Xeric is free software under the GNU Affero General Public License v3.0. The AGPL is chosen for its network clause: the system is built to run on the owner's machine, but it is a web application, and a hosted fork that closed the source would void the local-first promise the license exists to keep. The world itself is not the software — the template, the database, and everything a cast ever said belong to the person who forged them, and no license term reaches them. The repository [1] is the artifact this paper describes.
